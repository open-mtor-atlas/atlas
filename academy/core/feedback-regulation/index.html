Feedback Regulation

Why inhibiting mTORC1 can make signalling upstream of it go up

The question

Why does a pathway push back on its own input?

The core idea

Everything so far has run in one direction: inputs converge, mTORC1 responds, outputs change. Real signalling networks are not one-directional, and this one has a well-described loop running backwards. mTORC1 output weakens the growth-factor input that activated it.

The best-characterised route runs through S6K1. Active mTORC1 activates S6K1; S6K1 phosphorylates IRS-1, the adaptor that couples the insulin receptor to PI3K, and reduces its function and its abundance. A second route was found later by phosphoproteomics: mTORC1 phosphorylates Grb10, stabilising it as an inhibitor of insulin and IGF-1 signalling. Two mechanisms, the same sign.

This changes what an inhibitor experiment means. If mTORC1 activity is what suppresses the upstream branch, then removing mTORC1 activity releases that suppression, and signalling through PI3K and AKT can rise rather than fall. That is not an oddity of one cell line; it is the expected behaviour of a negative feedback loop with the loop opened, and it is a large part of why the clinical story of mTOR-directed drugs has been more complicated than the pathway diagram suggests.

The mechanism, in outline

Trace the loop on the left of the figure. PI3K and AKT activate mTORC1 (Lesson 07); mTORC1 activates S6K1; S6K1 acts on IRS-1; weakened IRS-1 means less signal from the insulin receptor into PI3K. Sustained mTORC1 activity therefore produces a cell that responds less well to insulin — cellular insulin resistance arising from the growth pathway itself rather than from anything wrong with the receptor.

Two companion papers established this in 2004 from opposite directions: one showing that loss of the TSC complex, which leaves mTORC1 and S6K1 constitutively active, disrupts insulin–PI3K signalling through IRS proteins; the other showing that inappropriate activation of the Rheb–mTOR–S6K cassette depletes IRS-1 and IRS-2 and produces cellular insulin resistance. Reading both is a good habit: independent groups, converging design, published within months.

The right of the figure is the same loop seen through a drug. Inhibiting mTORC1 in cancer cells relieves the feedback and activates upstream receptor-tyrosine-kinase and AKT signalling. A related route reaches the MAPK pathway: mTORC1 inhibition activates MAPK signalling through a PI3K-dependent feedback loop. Both are ways in which a drug aimed at a growth pathway can leave the cell with more proliferative signalling than before in some compartments.

There is also a whole-animal version of the problem with a different mechanism. In mice, chronic rapamycin disrupts mTORC2 as well and produces insulin resistance — and, importantly, that metabolic effect could be uncoupled from the lifespan extension. Feedback and off-target complex disruption are two distinct explanations for why the same drug produces both benefits and metabolic costs; distinguishing them matters for anyone trying to keep one and drop the other.

How to think about loops without getting lost

A practical rule: whenever you read a result of the form 'inhibiting X increased Y', ask whether Y is upstream of X. If it is, the first hypothesis should be a released feedback loop, not a paradox and not an off-target effect. The second question is the timescale — feedback release is often fast and transient, and a measurement at one hour and a measurement at one week can point in opposite directions. One study in this lesson found biphasic AKT regulation after mTOR kinase inhibition for exactly that reason.

The reverse rule is just as useful. If a paper reports that a treatment raises mTORC1 signalling over days, ask what happened to the upstream branch, because sustained mTORC1 activity should be eroding its own input. A result that shows both high mTORC1 output and undiminished insulin signalling over time is telling you something is unusual about the system, and that is worth understanding rather than skipping.

There is a design consequence too. Because the loop exists, an experiment that measures only one node of the pathway is measuring a moving target. This is one of the reasons phospho-S6 alone is a weak readout for 'mTOR activity' — it reports one substrate of one complex at one moment, in a network arranged to compensate.

Scope of the evidence here

The feedback mechanisms below were characterised in cultured cells, several of them cancer cell lines chosen because their signalling is strong and easy to read. The mouse study is tier C. None of this is a description of what happens in a person taking one of these drugs, and the corpus's human evidence on metabolic effects is a separate literature with its own tiers. Keep the mechanism and the clinical claim in separate boxes.

Think

A tumour has an activating change in PI3K. On the diagram, mTORC1 is downstream, so an mTORC1 inhibitor looks like the obvious move. Using this lesson, give two reasons the result might disappoint.

One reason is about the loop; one is about what the drug does and does not block.

Think first, then reveal

First, releasing feedback: removing mTORC1 output removes S6K1-mediated suppression of IRS-1 and Grb10-mediated suppression of insulin/IGF-1 signalling, so AKT signalling can rise — in a cell already driven by an activating PI3K change, that is the branch you least wanted to strengthen. A related route raises MAPK signalling through a PI3K-dependent loop. Second, rapamycin does not block all mTORC1 outputs equally; substrates such as 4E-BP1 are less sensitive than S6K1, so 'mTORC1 inhibited' on a phospho-S6 blot can coexist with continued cap-dependent translation. Both reasons argue for measuring more than one substrate and more than one time point, and both are why combination approaches and active-site inhibitors were pursued.

Chronic rapamycin in mice produces insulin resistance, and the same work found that this could be uncoupled from lifespan extension. Why is ‘uncoupled’ a stronger and more useful word here than ‘unrelated’?

Think about what an uncoupling result licenses you to try next.

Think first, then reveal

'Unrelated' would be a claim about mechanism — that the two outcomes share no causal machinery — which the experiment does not support. 'Uncoupled' is a claim about what was observed: conditions exist in which one appears without the other. That is weaker, and much more useful, because it converts a resignation ('the side effect is the price of the benefit') into a design problem ('find the conditions that separate them'). It is also directly testable, and the Atlas carries it as an open question rather than a settled result. Notice how much of the epistemic content of the finding sits in one word choice.
