Rheb — The Critical Regulator

A small GTPase, a brake, and a question about where things are

The question

How does a small GTPase help control mTORC1?

The core idea

Rheb is a small GTPase — a protein that cycles between a GTP-bound state and a GDP-bound state, and that behaves differently depending on which one it is in. In the standard model of mTORC1 activation, Rheb-GTP is the direct positive input to the kinase, and Rheb-GDP is not.

What controls that cycle is the TSC complex (TSC1, TSC2 and TBC1D7). TSC2 acts as a GAP — a GTPase-activating protein — for Rheb: it accelerates the conversion of Rheb-GTP to Rheb-GDP. A GAP for a positive regulator is a brake. Removing the brake raises Rheb-GTP and raises mTORC1 activity.

That is the mechanism in three moving parts, and it is also where growth-factor signalling enters the pathway: AKT, activated downstream of insulin and IGF-1, phosphorylates TSC2 and reduces the brake's effect on Rheb. Growth factors do not switch mTORC1 on. They release something that was holding it down.

The mechanism, in outline

The chain runs: growth factors → AKT ⊣ TSC1/TSC2 ⊣ Rheb-GTP → mTORC1. Note the two inhibitory steps in a row. A double negative is easy to misread, so it is worth saying slowly: AKT inhibits the TSC complex; the TSC complex inhibits Rheb; therefore AKT activity raises Rheb-GTP.

The TSC complex is also where several other inputs converge. Energy stress acting through AMPK, and various other stress signals, act on the same brake — which makes TSC a genuine integration point rather than a simple growth-factor relay.

But nucleotide state is only half the story, and this is the part most summaries drop.

Why location matters as much as state

Rheb is associated with intracellular membranes, prominently the lysosome. mTORC1 is not permanently at the lysosome — it is recruited there, and the recruitment is controlled by a separate machine: the Rag GTPases, held in place by the Ragulator complex, responding to amino-acid availability.

So the two branches do different jobs. The amino-acid branch decides where mTORC1 is. The growth-factor branch, through TSC and Rheb, decides whether the activator it meets there is in its active state. Neither alone is enough, which is a tidier explanation of the AND-gate behaviour from Lesson 01 than 'two signals add up'.

The spatial picture also runs in the other direction. The TSC complex itself can be recruited to the lysosome in response to cellular stress — putting the brake where the activator is, rather than acting at a distance. Localisation is not a detail layered on top of the mechanism; in this pathway it is part of the mechanism.

What is still uncertain

How much of mTORC1 regulation is nucleotide state, how much is localisation, and how much is the amount of protein present is not fully resolved, and the answer may differ between cell types and between acute and chronic conditions. Several of the classic experiments used overexpression, which is informative about what a protein can do and weaker evidence about what it normally does at endogenous levels.

This matters beyond the diagram. The TSC–Rheb axis is the axis broken in tuberous sclerosis complex, and it is the axis every mTOR-directed drug is acting somewhere near. How faithfully the arrows above describe an untouched human cell is a live question, not a settled one.

Think

What would you expect if Rheb were held predominantly in its GDP-bound state?

Work forwards from the arrow, then ask what else in the cell would notice.

Think first, then reveal

The direct expectation is low mTORC1 activity even when amino acids are plentiful — mTORC1 would be recruited to the lysosome and find no active activator waiting. Downstream, reduced S6K1 and 4E-BP1 phosphorylation, reduced cap-dependent translation, and released restraint on ULK1 and autophagy. The second-order expectation is more interesting: because mTORC1 output feeds back onto upstream insulin signalling, chronically low mTORC1 would also change AKT signalling — so the cell would not look like 'the same cell with one arrow deleted'.

What experiment could distinguish whether mTORC1 regulation here depends mainly on nucleotide state, on localisation, on protein abundance, or on a combination?

Each candidate implies a different thing you would have to hold constant.

Think first, then reveal

There is no single decisive experiment, which is itself the answer worth taking away. A serious attempt separates the variables: nucleotide-state mutants of Rheb locked toward GTP or GDP at endogenous expression levels (knock-in rather than overexpression, so abundance is held constant); forced targeting of mTORC1 to the lysosome independently of amino acids, to ask whether localisation alone is sufficient; and quantitative measurement of Rheb, TSC2 and mTOR protein amounts in the same cells, since a change in any of the three could produce the same readout. Combining them under matched conditions is what the field has actually done piecemeal over two decades — and the pieces were done in different cell types, which is part of why the question is still live.
