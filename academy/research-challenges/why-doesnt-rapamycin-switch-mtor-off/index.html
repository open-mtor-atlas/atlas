Why rapamycin does not switch mTORC1 off

One drug, two complexes, and a clock — and how you would tell which of the three you are looking at

Cells treated with rapamycin keep doing some of the things mTORC1 drives. Is that because of how the drug inhibits, because of what the experiment measures, because the network answers back, or because the drug is acting somewhere else?

This is a decision environment, not a quiz. Several of the choices below are scientifically defensible, and the feedback tells you what each one buys and what it costs rather than marking it right or wrong.

What you should be able to do

By the end of this challenge you should be able to

  • Distinguish a claim about a drug's target from a claim about which of that target's outputs it blocks
  • Predict which readout would hide a partial block and which would expose it
  • Rank candidate experiments by how well they separate a property of the drug from a response of the network
  • Identify what a single-arm dose-escalation trial can and cannot support

Research skills Causal reasoning · Experimental design · Control selection · Evidence evaluation · Alternative explanations · Scientific uncertainty

Step 1

What we know

Every line here is carried by a study in the Atlas, linked beside it. Read the second list as carefully as the first: it is where your own decisions start.

What the Atlas already supports

  • Rapamycin does not work alone. It binds the small abundant protein FKBP12, and the pair docks on a site next to the kinase's active site rather than in it, so the inhibition is steric obstruction rather than occupancy of the catalytic pocket. CHO1996 SAB1995
  • The S6K1 arm goes quiet within minutes of treatment, and that band is the readout most papers use when they report ‘mTORC1 activity’. CHU1992 PRI1992
  • Compared against an inhibitor that occupies the active site, rapamycin leaves a class of mTORC1 outputs largely running — 4E-BP1 phosphorylation most of all, and autophagy induction as well. THO2009 FEL2009
  • Time changes the target list. Short exposure leaves the second complex alone; prolonged exposure traps free mTOR and new mTORC2 stops being assembled in the sensitive lines, and the same loss of signalling appears in mouse liver. SAR2006 LAM2012
  • Inhibiting mTORC1 releases a brake on the arm above it: S6K1 no longer represses IRS-1, upstream receptor signalling picks up, and Akt activity can rise rather than fall. HAR2004 ORE2006 ROD2011
  • A bivalent molecule can reach the substrate rapamycin leaves alone while leaving the second complex largely engaged, in resistant breast-cancer models and in a first-in-human dose-escalation trial. MEN2023 SCH2025

Where that stops

  • Whether prolonged exposure reaches the second complex in any particular cell depends on the line, the dose and the duration. The Atlas grades that edge as contested for a reason: it is absent acutely and variably present later.
  • Two routes lead from mTORC1 inhibition to disturbed glucose handling — loss of the second complex and relief of the S6K1-to-IRS-1 brake — and their relative contribution has never been apportioned in people.
  • The lifespan data in this corpus come from rapamycin, and the selective compounds have been read out against tumour endpoints. No study here joins selective inhibition to an ageing outcome, which is the join the last sub-question asks for.

Rapamycin
Allosteric mTORC1 inhibitor that works only as a complex with FKBP12.The drug that made this pathway famous.Sirolimus. ATP-competitive mTOR inhibitors
Torin1, AZD8055 and relatives occupy the mTOR active site directly, inhibiting both complexes.Drugs that block mTOR's engine instead of getting in its way.Designed as a research answer to rapamycin's incompleteness, then developed clinically. Because they compete with ATP at the catalytic site they do not need FKBP12, they suppress 4E-BP1 phosphorylation that rapalogs leave standing, and they hit mTORC2 as well – which is both their advantage and their toxicity problem.

mTORC1

mTORC2

S6K1

4E-BP1

ULK1

Akt/PKB

eIF4E
Cap-binding translation initiation factor.The clamp that starts reading mRNA.Rate-limiting for cap-dependent initiation; a proto-oncogene in its own right when overexpressed.
Autophagy

Note the direction: the loop degrades IRS-1 when mTORC1 is ACTIVE, so it is a mechanism of insulin resistance under nutrient excess. Inhibiting mTORC1 releases it and raises Akt signalling instead.

Cap-dependent translation, the most direct mTORC1 output.Making new proteins.Controlled through 4E-BP/eIF4E and S6K1/PDCD4/eIF4A arms; ribosome-profiling shows the response is transcript-selective, not uniform.

Set the drug to rapamycin and the clock to acute. Two of the three output arms are in the model. Which readout would report the pathway as switched off, and which would report it still running?

Step 3

Commit to a working hypothesis

Commit to a working hypothesis before you spend anything. It is meant to be revised later — that is what the second half of this challenge is for.

What each choice commits you to

The block is partial by construction. The drug obstructs the doorway to the kinase rather than filling its catalytic pocket, so substrates that need deep, sustained access lose out and others carry on. It predicts that a chemistry which does occupy the site reaches further, and that the split between outputs is reproducible rather than noise. Its weak point: obstruction and network feedback predict the same blot in a single-time-point experiment.

The incompleteness is in the readout, not the drug. On this reading mTORC1 really is switched off and the persisting signal is an artefact of which band gets blotted, of antibody sensitivity, or of a phosphosite that another kinase also touches. It predicts that a wider substrate panel, or a functional readout instead of a phosphosite, makes the residue disappear. It is the hypothesis most worth holding early and the easiest to hold too long.

The network answers back. Block the kinase and the loops around it re-set: S6K1 stops repressing IRS-1, upstream receptor signalling picks up, and the cell you measure is no longer the untreated cell with one arrow cut. It predicts that the residue grows over time rather than staying flat, and that the arm above the kinase moves in the same lysates. It has to explain why a deeper chemistry, which relieves the same feedback, still gives a deeper block.

The interesting part is the complex the drug was not supposed to touch. Here the incompleteness on mTORC1 is the less consequential half of the story, and what matters is that prolonged exposure reaches the second complex, taking the arm to Akt with it. It predicts that the drug's effects at day ten are not the drug's effects at minute ten. Its weak point as an answer to the question asked: it explains the side effects better than it explains the residue.

Run the investigation

    What each choice commits you to

    It supports my hypothesis. For partial block, strongly, and with a scope worth stating: two chemistries, one panel of substrates, in cells. What it does not give you is the size of that difference in a tissue.

    It weakens my hypothesis. For readout-artefact it is close to decisive: the residue moves when the molecule changes while the antibodies stay the same, which is not how a reagent artefact behaves.

    What this evidence supports

    • A deeper block is chemically available, so what rapamycin leaves running is a property of how rapamycin inhibits rather than a limit of the kinase.
    • The rapamycin-resistant outputs are only visible when a second chemistry is there to measure against — which is why the field lived with them for a decade.

    What it does not establish

    • That the deeper block is the better drug. This design reads substrate phosphorylation, not benefit and not harm.
    • Which complex each compound is acting on: the same active site sits in both, so this chemistry has no reason to tell them apart.

    Information value The most discriminating step at a middling price.     The short answer

    On the evidence in this Atlas: because of how it inhibits, and then, after long enough, because of where it ends up.     Observation — what has actually been measured

    • Rapamycin binds FKBP12 and the pair docks on a site adjacent to the kinase's active site rather than in it. CHO1996 SAB1995

    • Against an active-site inhibitor as a ruler, rapamycin leaves 4E-BP1 phosphorylation largely in place and induces autophagy only partly. THO2009 FEL2009
    • Inhibiting mTORC1 relieves feedback onto receptor signalling, and Akt phosphorylation can rise rather than fall. ORE2006 HAR2004 ROD2011

    • A bi-steric molecule suppresses the rapamycin-resistant substrate while sparing the second complex, in resistant tumour models and in a single-arm dose-escalation trial. MEN2023 SCH2025

    Interpretation — how the field reads it

    Read together, these support a two-axis picture rather than a single number for potency. Chemistry decides the depth of the block on mTORC1 outputs: obstruction of the substrate channel leaves some substrates reachable, and occupying the active site does not. Duration decides its breadth: acutely one complex, after prolonged exposure often both. Most apparent disagreements about ‘how much rapamycin inhibits mTOR’ turn out to be two groups reporting from different corners of those two axes.

    The network's reply is a real third contributor and not a rival to the first. Relieving the brake on the arm above the kinase happens under either chemistry, so it cannot be why one reaches further — but it does mean a treated cell is not an untreated cell with one arrow cut, and it sets how fast a partly blocked signal creeps back.

    What the drug does, then, is not to turn mTORC1 output down by a fixed fraction but to reshape which outputs stay on. That reshaping is a plausible reason the same molecule can extend lifespan in mice while performing modestly as a single agent against many tumours, and it is why ‘rapamycin induces autophagy’ needs the dose, the cell type and the readout attached before it is a claim.

    What each choice commits you to

    Run a second-complex-sparing molecule against a survival endpoint, with rapamycin alongside at matched target engagement. The direct attack on the gap, and the most expensive option on any realistic menu. Budget both sexes and the rapamycin arm from the start: without the comparator the survival curve cannot separate selectivity from dose, and without both sexes it answers a narrower question than the one asked.

    Apportion the two routes to disturbed glucose handling in one set of animals. Cheaper and badly needed. Loss of the second complex and relief of the S6K1-to-IRS-1 brake have never been weighed against each other in the same experiment, and the answer would tell you whether a selective molecule can spare the metabolic cost or only part of it.

    Map the residue across a wide substrate panel and a dose-response, in more than one cell type. The unglamorous option that would firm up the foundation. Much of what we call rapamycin-resistant output rests on a handful of substrates in a handful of lines; knowing which substrates behave that way, and how reproducibly, would sharpen every design downstream.

    Read the second-complex effect across lines, doses and durations to find where it actually appears. Follows the most contested edge in this story rather than the original question. The Atlas grades it contested because sensitivity varies; a systematic map would turn a caveat into a rule of thumb, which is worth more than it sounds for anyone choosing a schedule.
